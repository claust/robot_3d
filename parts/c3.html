Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-C5

ok×2

Thumbnail-sized ESP32-C5 board on the XIAO form factor, USB-C, with dual-band Wi-Fi 6 (2.4 and 5 GHz), Bluetooth 5 LE and IEEE 802.15.4 (Thread/Zigbee/Matter) on one chip. Two in hand. One of them is already flashed and running esp32-c5/blink; the second has not been powered up yet.

The interesting role is as the robot's motor-side MCU: it has real hardware PWM, which the Pi Zero 2 W (C1) does not — the Pi has two PWM channels shared across pins, so the four DRV8833 inputs run on DMA-timed software PWM. A C5 can own the driver and the timing loop while the Pi keeps the WiFi UI, or replace it outright on a smaller build.

  • 32-bit single-core RISC-V up to 240 MHz, 384 KB SRAM, 320 KB ROM. 8 MB flash — confirmed on our board with esptool flash-id, and set in esp32-c5/blink/sdkconfig.defaults because the IDF default is 2 MB. Seeed also list 8 MB PSRAM; not confirmed here yet.
  • Radio goes out through a U.FL connector to the supplied FPC antenna — there is no PCB antenna to fall back on, so a board with nothing plugged in will look like a dead radio rather than a weak one. The 5 GHz band is the reason to prefer this over the C6/C3 XIAOs: the 2.4 GHz band around the bench is crowded, and the Pi (C1) is 2.4 GHz only.
  • 3.3 V logic, same as the Pi: drives the DRV8833 inputs (D2/D3) directly, since the DRV8833's VIH is 2 V regardless of its motor rail. It will not talk to the 5 V I²C of the SRF08 (S1) or the MD22 (D1) without a level shifter — that is still the Uno's (C2) job.
  • 14 pads along the two long edges, 2.54 mm pitch: D0–D10 (11 GPIO, all PWM-capable) plus 5V, GND, 3V3. Castellated and drilled, so the board can take headers or be reflowed flat onto a carrier.
  • Pin map, from third-party pinout references and not yet checked against our boards: D0=GPIO1, D1=GPIO0, D2=GPIO25, D3=GPIO7, D4=GPIO23 (SDA), D5=GPIO24 (SCL), D6=GPIO11 (TX), D7=GPIO12 (RX), D8=GPIO8, D9=GPIO9, D10 unconfirmed. I²C on D4/D5, UART on D6/D7, SPI on D8/D9/D10. Blink each pad before trusting any of it.
  • User LED on GPIO 27, active-low — driving the pin low lights it. That one is confirmed on our board; see esp32-c5/blink/main/blink_main.c.
  • Underside pads (bare SMD, no header footprint): B+ and B- for a 3.7 V LiPo, the JTAG bonding pads, and four extra ADC inputs A1–A4. Battery charging is an onboard SGM40567 at ~100 mA whenever USB is in, with a TPS22916 gating the battery-voltage sense.
  • Power in: USB-C, the 5V pad, or a cell on B+/B-. B+ is a battery node, not a supply input — feed the board from the MP1584 buck (P1) on the 5V pad, not onto the charger. Deep sleep is quoted at ~15 µA, which is the one number that would make a sleep-and-wake sensor node on this platform worthwhile.
  • USB-C is the chip's own USB Serial/JTAG, not a CP2102-style bridge: console, flashing and debugging all go down the one cable, and the port is exclusive — close the Arduino or VS Code monitor before reading it from a shell.

Soldering to the underside pads — the only way to reach the battery, JTAG or A1–A4 — wants a bit of care, because the pad is the weak link and it lifts long before the wire breaks:

  • Flux the pad, tin pad and wire separately, then reflow the two together. Two seconds per joint, iron on pad and wire, same rule as the D2 headers in cad/robot_car/WIRING.md.
  • 30 AWG silicone-jacketed stranded, or enamelled magnet wire. Anything heavier levers the pad off the laminate.
  • Strain-relieve at the joint (hot-glue or UV-resin fillet), or route the wire through a hole in the printed mount so nothing pulls on copper.
  • Continuity-check B+ against B- with a meter before a cell goes anywhere near it: a solder bridge there is a LiPo short, not a blown fuse.
  • Prefer removable where possible — a short JST-PH pigtail glued down for the battery, pogo pins in a printed jig for JTAG. And with the console already on USB Serial/JTAG, the JTAG pads may never be needed at all.

Toolchain is ESP-IDF v6.1 installed through EIM under ~/.espressif; from esp32-c5/blink: source ~/.espressif/tools/activate_idf_v6.1.sh, then idf.py build and idf.py -p /dev/cu.usbmodem* flash. In VS Code open robot_3d.code-workspace, not the bare repo.

To verify:

  • Second board: enumerate over USB-C and flash blink — clears the board, the cable and the USB Serial/JTAG path in one go
  • Confirm the D0–D10 → GPIO map by blinking each pad in turn, and pin down what D10 is
  • Whether PSRAM is actually present and how much (idf.py size / an esp_psram probe at boot)
  • Caliper the outline, thickness, pad pitch and row spacing, plus U.FL connector height with the antenna plugged in — all needed before a CAD mount
  • Check an antenna is fitted, then compare 2.4 vs 5 GHz association and range from the far end of the flat
  • Current draw at 3.3 V: idle, Wi-Fi associated, 5 GHz TX burst, and deep sleep — the quoted 15 µA is the claim to test
  • LEDC hardware PWM on four pins into a DRV8833 (D2), scoped or LED-checked, before any motor is connected
  • Battery path: pigtail onto B+/B-, confirm the ~100 mA charge current and whether cell voltage can be read back in firmware
  • Decide the split with the Pi (C1) — C5 as a motor/timing co-processor over UART, or as the whole brain on a smaller robot
